# Product Brief - Wardrobe Planner

**Team Name:** MisFits AI  
**Date:** February 24, 2026

---

## The Problem

Busy students and professionals wake up, stare at their closet, and waste time deciding what to wear for the day ahead. The decision is not just "what looks good?" It depends on the weather, meetings, commute, social plans, comfort, and what is actually clean and available.

They currently solve this by checking the weather, skimming Google Calendar, mentally filtering their wardrobe, texting friends or partners, or falling back to the same few "safe" outfits. That creates morning decision fatigue, outfit repetition, and anxiety about being underdressed, overdressed, or visually inconsistent with the situation.

This pain showed up repeatedly in interviews through comments like "You never know what's in your wardrobe," "Sometimes I wear my cargos too much," and "I have a default that I will go back to."

---

## The Solution

Wardrobe Planner is an outfit planning assistant that connects a user's calendar, local weather, and wardrobe preferences to suggest complete, ready-to-wear outfits for real days.

The user connects Google Calendar and location once, sets a lightweight wardrobe baseline, and then receives a small set of outfit suggestions each morning or the night before. They can accept a suggestion, swap one piece, or ignore it. The product is designed to reduce mental overhead without taking away the user's agency.

Unlike generic style inspiration or shopping recommendations, Wardrobe Planner works from the user's actual schedule, actual weather, and actual wardrobe constraints.

---

## Target User

Wardrobe Planner is for calendar-driven students and professionals, roughly ages 22-38, who care about looking put together but do not want to spend 10-20 minutes deciding what to wear.

The strongest target segments from interviews and synthetic testing are:

- **Vibe Coder Victor:** Tech and knowledge workers who default to hoodies and jeans but want to look more intentional at work without becoming "fashion people."
- **Resident Rachel:** Residents and essential workers who live in scrubs or uniforms and want off-duty outfits to feel functional, adult, and low effort.
- **Recently Promoted Priya:** Newly promoted managers whose wardrobe has not caught up to their role.
- **Hybrid Office Owen:** Hybrid workers who move between home, office, commute, and after-work plans.
- **Light-Touch Styler Sam:** People who do not treat fashion as identity but still care about not looking sloppy.

These users typically have mid-range closets, use Google Calendar regularly, check the weather most mornings, and feel like they are underusing clothes they already own.

---

## Why Now

People already organize their lives through digital calendars, but wardrobe decisions are still manual and disconnected from the systems that know what the day looks like. Weather is also a daily variable, especially in cities like New York, Chicago, and London, where commuting and temperature swings make outfit planning more complicated.

AI personalization now makes it feasible to turn scattered context - calendar, forecast, wardrobe preferences, and user feedback - into practical recommendations. The opportunity is not to create a fashion makeover tool, but to make everyday dressing feel less effortful.

---

## Key Interview Evidence

**Strongest quote:** "You never know what's in your wardrobe." This captured the core memory and visibility problem: people own usable clothes, but cannot easily recall or combine them when making quick decisions.

**Pattern:** Multiple interviewees relied on a narrow set of default outfits even when they owned more clothes. They made wardrobe decisions quickly and mentally, often with no system other than memory, which led to repetition and uncertainty.

**Surprise:** Some users resisted fully automated styling because they still wanted control and creativity. That pushed the product toward suggestions, swaps, and lightweight guidance rather than "we decide for you."

**Current spend/effort:** Users already spend time browsing stores, trying items in person, texting friends for opinions, and buying items that later do not match the rest of their wardrobe.

## Open Questions

1. Will users who rely on default outfits adopt automated suggestions if the system preserves control and feels personal?
2. How lightweight can onboarding be while still producing trustworthy outfit suggestions?
3. Should the first version prioritize daily outfit decisions, shopping decisions, or both?
4. What proof is needed for users to believe the system understands their real wardrobe rather than producing generic outfit templates?
5. Which pricing model best matches the perceived value: free, low-cost subscription, or paid premium features?
